The question

Do individual sheep matter for where the flock goes?

Individual differences, leadership and sheep flocking models are already well studied. The open question here is narrower and testable: do a sheep's own responsiveness and its independently measured relationships improve predictions of unseen movement, and can they be measured reliably at all?

ri

Responsiveness

How strongly sheep i turns toward the average heading of its neighbours. 0 ignores them; 1 copies them.

An independent ewe might sit near 0.2, a strong follower near 0.9.

Aij

Relationship

How much sheep i attends to one particular partner j. Measured from observations that are independent of the movement being predicted.

A ewe attends closely to her lamb and barely to a stranger.

qj

Influence

How strongly sheep j is followed by everyone around it. Estimated only if the data can separate it from relationships.

An older, experienced ewe may be followed more widely.

The movement rule for sheep i, each time step new heading = (1 − ri) · own heading + ri · Σj Aij qj · headingj / Σj Aij qj

Neighbours are every sheep within a fixed radius. A little random noise η is added to each decision. With equal responsiveness, uniform ties and q = 1, the rule reduces exactly to the classic Vicsek flocking model, and an automated test checks that it does.

What movement alone cannot tell us

Popular or loud? The data cannot say.

Each neighbour counts by the product Aij × qj. Double every tie toward j, halve j's influence, and every prediction stays identical. So from trajectories alone, a sheep everyone is closely tied to and a sheep that is simply followed are indistinguishable.

The repository proves this with a test and keeps influence out of the main models unless independent information separates the two. Reporting a precise number that means nothing is the failure this design avoids.

The model ladder

Add one idea at a time. Keep it only if it predicts.

Every model uses the same movement rule, data split and scoring. Each rung adds a single biological idea. If a rung does not beat the one below on held-out groups or days, the idea is not supported, and that is a valid result.

M1 against M0Does knowing each sheep's responsiveness help?
M2 against M0 and distance-onlyDo relationships help beyond who is simply nearest?
M3 against M1 and M2Do the two ideas add information the other lacks?
M3 against shuffled networksIs it the real relationships, or any network of the same shape?

Fair testing rules: whole groups, bouts or days are held out, never neighbouring GPS fixes; uncertainty is counted per held-out unit with a paired block bootstrap; and models must also beat persistence, constant-turn and distance-only controls.

Evidence so far

What the method shows, on models and on real sheep.

Stored simulations show how heading order responds to individual differences, a recovery study shows how much tracking error the method can tolerate, and a run on public sheep data shows what the full pipeline concludes from real animals.

Heading order falls once sheep differ enough

Legacy approximate-search runs

200 simulated sheep, mean responsiveness 0.7, 20 runs at each of 57 settings. The flock stays aligned while differences are small, then order drops quickly. More noise makes the drop come earlier.

Read with care. These runs used an approximate neighbour search, so the realised interaction radius was not exactly 1. The code now uses exact search and the full sweep needs re-running. An earlier phase-transition claim was withdrawn after audit: 323 of 1,520 size-comparison runs were still drifting.

Small tracking errors distort individual estimates

Synthetic Seed 2026, 3 replicates, 45 individual fits per scenario; true responsiveness 0.15 to 0.85; steps of 0.65 m.

Synthetic groups with known responsiveness were degraded with position error, missing fixes and slower sampling, then fitted by the same pipeline used for real data.

With exact positions the fit recovers each sheep closely and in the right order. Just 5 cm of error makes sheep look more responsive than they are, and at 25 cm the ranking of sheep is lost.

Many ordinary GPS collars have errors of metres. Sensor precision relative to how far a sheep moves between fixes therefore decides whether individual differences can be measured at all.

Applied to 14 real Merino sheep

Public tracking data from 14 ewes herded by a dog (Jadhav et al. 2024, Communications Biology), run through the repository pipeline with settings fixed in a written protocol before any model was fitted. Each of 8 trials is held out in turn.

Copying neighbours' headings clearly predicts better than keeping one's own heading. Giving each sheep its own responsiveness, or weighting neighbours by ties measured in a separate trial, adds little or nothing at 0.5 s and 1 s. At 2 s individual responsiveness gives a small, consistent gain.

Read with care. All 8 trials involve the same 14 sheep, so intervals are exploratory. Ties are spatial associations from an independent trial, not observed affiliations. The dog is not modelled.
